The carbon impact of everyday choices

Our intuition for carbon is terrible — we sweat the tiny stuff and wave through the enormous. So let’s put the choices a person actually makes onto one honest scale and see how they really compare. The numbers come from two of the most-cited studies on the question — Murtaugh & Schlax (2009), “Reproduction and the carbon legacies of individuals”, and Wynes & Nicholas (2017), “The climate mitigation gap” — re-run here with current US data so you can check them and push the assumptions yourself.

A sense of scale

Take one person and put everything they could change onto a single axis — tonnes of CO₂e per year. Each green bar is a yearly emission that person could avoid. The familiar lifestyle changes (Wynes & Nicholas, 2017) sit in the middle; the per-use things people fret about are at the floor, annualized at deliberately heavy rates. The biggest levers are live outputs of the model below and will shift as you adjust its assumptions.

The model behind the numbers

The biggest results above come from one place: Murtaugh & Schlax’s model for the carbon legacy of a single marginal child. They hold a person responsible for a share of their descendants’ emissions, weighted by relatedness: a descendant n generations removed counts as (½)n of a “genetic unit”. Here’s their model, live — replicate the 2008 paper, swap in current US data, or push the fertility and emission projections yourself and watch every number on the page update.

Appendix — the immigration comparison

The “Doesn’t immigrate to the US” saving up top is a single number: the carbon-legacy gap between the average US foreign-born person (measured fertility 2.19, US emissions) and the average person in immigrants’ origin countries (origin-weighted fertility ~1.9, ~3.8 t emissions). The highlighted row below is exactly that. The country rows show the mechanism — a marginal adult and their genetic-unit-weighted line emitting at the US rate (≈13.8 t/person/yr) instead of the home rate — holding each origin’s own fertility fixed on both sides, so those increases are the pure emissions-intensity jump. Methodology & caveats

Model. Analytical model of Appendix A. Survivorship S(a) is a one-parameter Gompertz–Makeham curve calibrated so its integral equals the chosen life expectancy — a smooth approximation of NCHS life tables, accurate near US life expectancy (~80 yr) and progressively rougher at lower values. Net reproduction R(t) = TFR(t) · ffemale · S(T). Emissions and fertility each follow a linear path from the base year to a target, then hold constant. Generation time T defaults to 30 yr and the female fraction to 0.5, calibrated so the US 2005 inputs reproduce the paper’s 9441 t (model: ~9663, +2.4%) and 470 person-years (model: ~479).

Convergence. The per-child sum converges only when fertility eventually drops below replacement (R<1); sustained at-or-above-replacement fertility makes the lineage — and the legacy — grow without bound, which the page flags. This is why the paper’s per-child numbers use declining UN-medium-variant fertility.

Caveat (the paper’s own). These are individual carbon legacies with intentional double-counting across ancestors — each ancestor is assigned a weighted share of shared descendants — not population totals. Results are highly sensitive to the emission scenario.

Current US figures. TFR ≈ 1.60 (CDC/NCHS: 2023 = 1.621, 2024 = 1.599, an all-time low) vs. 2.05 in 2005. US energy-related CO₂ per capita fell ~30% from 2005 to 2023 (≈20 → ≈14 t/person/yr; EIA).
